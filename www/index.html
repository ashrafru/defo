A workbench, at your pace.

Switch freely. Open a tool from the library. Tap Open to move between running tools. Each keeps its inputs and results.

Follow two leads. Tap Compare to show two tools at once: stacked on a phone held upright, side by side when turned or on a tablet. Drag the bar between them to resize. New copy in a tool’s ⋯ menu opens an independent second instance.

Keep your favorites close. Star any tool to pin it at the top of the library.

Make room. Use the zoom control in a tool’s bar to fit wide charts and tables. You can also scroll sideways inside a tool.

Save inside the tool. Use each tool’s own save or export controls before closing it or the app. Open tools and unsaved input are not restored after closing.

Offline. A tool is stored on this device the first time you open it. More › Save all tools offline stores the full suite (about 64 MB). Calculations run on your device; city searches and external links need internet.

How The Astrologers’ Workbench came about
by James Alexander

A search for dependable methods

I was a long-time research astrologer looking for more accurate and reliable rectification methods. I approached astrology with a scientific spirit: calculations should be open to examination, methods should be tested against accurately dated events, and claims of precision should be supported by results. That search grew out of years of research and dissatisfaction with the methods I had encountered.

Meeting Isaac Starkman changed the direction of that search. His work with Topocentric Primary Directions and exact birthtimes showed me an approach that could be examined systematically, event by event and candidate time by candidate time. It changed how I understood rectification, and what I wanted from the software used to perform it.

From one HTML document to a suite

I was dismayed that such valuable, precise systems were available mainly on aging platforms or within very expensive software. I began with a straightforward goal: create an HTML document that could calculate Topocentric Primary Directions in a modern browser.

The next step was to follow Isaac’s approach of scanning candidate birthtimes and comparing their TPD performance across a biography. Incorporating his weighting matrix brought that work closer to an updated Polaris engine. The result, Polaris2, is a direct descendant of Isaac’s Polaris, adapted to run in a modern browser.

One program soon suggested others. A collection of auxiliary programs, each running as an HTML document, could support the whole research process. Many of my original software ideas became practical tools for examining events, comparing candidate times and exploring related techniques. Some of their most useful features grew from imaginative solutions and a willingness to approach familiar research problems in less conventional ways. I carried the project from that first calculator through the design and development of the suite, bringing these ideas and techniques together in a shared workbench. Juan Estadella collaborated on key techniques and contributed ideas that helped improve and extend the suite.

The research behind the tools

This project owes much to a line of researchers whose work deserves to remain accessible:

Carl Kühr
His correction to primary direction calculations is central to the methods used here.
Wendel Polich & A. P. Nelson Page
Their discovery of the topocentric house system, understood here as a discovery rather than an invention, provides a foundation for this work.
Alexander Marr
His meticulous research and insistence on reliability echo J. B. Morin’s practice of discarding methods that failed to demonstrate dependable results.
Isaac Starkman
His research, weighting matrix and remarkable rectification program Polaris made this approach possible. The suite’s Polaris2 carries that work into a modern browser.
Juan Estadella
His writings and teaching make these methods available to a wider audience. His collaboration has also helped shape techniques and enhancements within this suite.
James Alexander
His decades of research into rectification and predictive methods, original software ideas and sustained work developing the tools brought this suite together. His work explores practical ways to compare and evaluate exact techniques, with a commitment to making them freely accessible.
Fadi Mazboudi
A student of Juan’s, helping introduce these methods to others and carry them forward.

Given freely to the community

The Astrologers’ Workbench brings together 36 useful programs, offered freely to the astrological community. My purpose is to encourage interest in these golden methods, make their examination easier, and give more astrologers the tools to test, study and develop them.

James Alexander
in collaboration with Isaac Starkman and Juan Estadella
